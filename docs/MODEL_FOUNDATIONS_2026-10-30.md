## 1. The data

### 1.1 One row = one firm's annual accounts for one year

Think of each row as **one photocopy of a company's financial statements** for one year, plus a
few facts about the company.

| | |
|---|---|
| Rows | 1,023,552 |
| Firms | 237,711 (each firm appears at most once per year) |
| Years (`fs_year`) | 2007–2012 |
| Statement date (`stmt_date`) | Always 31 December of `fs_year` |
| Who's in it | Italian private, non-financial firms with total assets > €1.5M |
| Firms that ever default | 16,863 (7.1% of firms) |

Source: `reports/data/00_labels.md`, `reports/data/profile_by_year.csv`.

### 1.2 What the columns are (grouped so they make sense)

**Who the firm is (labels, never numbers):**
- `id`: firm identifier.
- `HQ_city`: a province code (Milano, Roma, …).
- `legal_struct`: legal form (SRL, SPA, …).
- `ateco_sector`: industry code.

**Balance sheet: what the firm owns and owes on Dec 31 (a snapshot):**
- **Owns (assets):** `asst_tang_fixed` (buildings, machines), `asst_intang_fixed` (brands,
  capitalised costs), `asst_fixed_fin` (financial investments), `asst_current` (stock,
  receivables, cash), with `AR` (money customers owe) and `cash_and_equiv` inside it. Total:
  `asst_tot`.
- **Owes (liabilities):** `debt_st` (due within 12 months), `debt_lt` (due later). Breakdown:
  `debt_bank_st/lt` (banks), `debt_fin_st/lt` (other lenders), `AP_st/lt` (suppliers).
- **Owner's money (equity):** `eqty_tot` = assets − liabilities. If negative, the firm owes more
  than it owns, on paper.

**Income statement: what happened during the year (a movie):**
- `rev_operating` (sales), `COGS` (cost of what was sold), `ebitda` (operating cash earnings),
  `prof_operations` (operating profit), `exp_financing` (interest paid), `profit` (net profit
  after everything).

**Pre-computed fields from the data provider:** `roa`, `roe`, `wc_net`, `margin_fin`,
`days_rec`, `cf_operations`.

### 1.3 Things in the data that are NOT what they look like (know these cold)

| Field | What the name says | What it actually is (found by checking the numbers) |
|---|---|---|
| `roa` | Return on assets (net) | **Operating** profit / assets × 100 (exact on 100% of rows) |
| `goodwill` | Goodwill | Behaves like **value added** (EBITDA ≤ it on 99.99% of rows) |
| `taxes` | Taxes | Equals **profit before tax** on 99.7% of rows |
| `inc_financing` | Financial income | Looks like the **net financial result** |
| `roe`, `margin_fin` | ROE, equity − fixed assets | **Blank whenever equity ≤ 0**, which is exactly the riskiest firms |
| `days_rec` | Days receivable | 72% missing and doesn't match AR/sales → not used |
| `eqty_corp_family_tot` | Group equity | 100% empty → dropped |
| Column names | Dictionary says `rev_operations`, `prof_operating`, `eqyt_tot` | File says `rev_operating`, `prof_operations`, `eqty_tot` → harness accepts both |

These are hypotheses from the data (D-010, D-024), so call them "we found that…", not "it is".
Source: `reports/data/01_profile.md`.

### 1.5 Missing data: why values are missing

1. **The debt breakdown** (bank / other lenders / suppliers) is missing **as a block** on about
   12% of rows (15.7% of 2007–09 training rows). The totals (`debt_st`, `debt_lt`) are still
   there. It's mostly small firms (13.6% vs 1.2% for large firms) and stays missing for the same
   firm year after year.
   - **Likely reason:** small Italian firms may file *abbreviated* accounts with fewer line items
     (Civil Code art. 2435-bis). Say "plausible", because the thresholds before 2008 aren't
     verified.
   - **Does it predict default?** Barely: 1.57% vs 1.38%.
2. **`roe` and `margin_fin` blank when equity ≤ 0.** Here missing **means** "negative equity":
   default rate 10.3% / 8.0% vs about 1%. Filling these blanks with an average would erase the
   strongest danger signal. So we don't use these fields; we rebuild the information from equity
   and assets.

### 1.6 Is this "only firms the bank lent to"? (selection bias)

The proposal said so; Roger asked "How do you know?". The data-auditor found 145k–186k firms a
year, in all 20 regions and 83 industry divisions. That looks more like **a broad sample of
Italian companies above €1.5M** than one bank's loan book. This is a hypothesis
(`01_profile.md` §7). **What to say:** "We can't verify that the data contains only booked loans;
if it did, PDs could be less reliable for applicants unlike past borrowers."

## 2. The problem: what we predict and how we test it

### 2.1 The label: "did the firm default in the window?"

`def_date` is set **per firm**: the same date is on every row of a firm that defaulted. Defaults
run from 2008-01 to 2013-05-31.

**Our rule (D-002):** a statement dated Dec 31 of year Y gets **y = 1** if the firm defaults
between **July 1 of Y+1 and June 30 of Y+2**. Otherwise y = 0.

Picture it for a 2010 statement:

```
Dec 31 2010          Jul 1 2011                         Jun 30 2012
    |-- 6 months: accounts approved & filed --|-------- 12-month window --------|
 statement date        bank can use it                    default here => y = 1
```

**Why wait 6 months?** In Italy, shareholders approve the accounts within **120 days** of
year-end (up to 180 if the statute allows), then the accounts are filed within **30 days**
(Civil Code arts. 2364, 2435; R-28, R-29). The bank can't lend on accounts that don't exist yet.
These are legal **maximums**, not observed filing dates; say so.

**Rows we drop:**
- 4,237 rows where the firm defaulted in those first 6 months. The bank could never have used
  that statement in time.
- 2011–2012 statements, whose window runs past May 2013. We can't know the outcome, so we don't
  pretend y = 0.

**Why it matters:** with a 0-month start, the 2007–10 default rate is 1.07%; with a 6-month
start it's 1.41%. **The window choice moves the PD level by about a third.** That's why we asked
Roger (email Q1).

### 2.2 How we test: walk-forward (like real life)

A bank builds a model on the past and uses it on the future. So we do the same:

| Fold | Train on | Test on | Test defaults |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2007 | 2008 | 2,064 |
| 2 | 2007–2008 | 2009 | 2,752 |
| 3 | 2007–2009 | 2010 | 2,968 |

**Rule:** anything the model learns (bin edges, averages, coefficients, calibration) is learned
from the training years only. The test year is touched once, to score.

### 2.3 How we score: two different questions

| Question | Metric | Plain meaning | Serves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does it **rank** firms right? | **AUC** | Pick a random defaulter and a random survivor; AUC = chance the model gave the defaulter the higher PD. 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect. (AR = 2·AUC − 1, same information) | Approve/decline |
| Are the PD **numbers** right? | **Calibration-in-the-large**, observed-vs-predicted table, **log loss** | If we say 2%, do about 2% default? Log loss punishes confident wrong numbers | Pricing |

Brier score = overall accuracy, **not** a calibration measure (R-16 p. 32).

**Uncertainty:** we bootstrap by **firm**, because a firm appears in several years. Treating its
rows as independent would overstate how sure we are.

## 3. How any model turns numbers into a PD (the one idea to understand)

Every model we built does the same three things:

1. Turn each ratio into a **risk signal**.
2. **Add up** the signals into one **score** (on a "log-odds" scale).
3. Turn the score into a **probability** between 0 and 1.

**Log-odds in one minute.** Odds = PD / (1 − PD). A 1% PD is odds of 1:99. Log-odds = ln(odds):

| PD | log-odds |
|---|---|
| 0.1% | −6.9 |
| 1% | −4.6 |
| 2% | −3.9 |
| 10% | −2.2 |
| 50% | 0 |

Adding **+0.7** to the log-odds roughly **doubles** a small PD. That's why models add things up
on this scale: risk factors multiply the odds.

### Worked example: score one firm by hand (runnable)

Fictional firm "Rossi Srl": manufacturing, limited company (SRL), North-West Italy. The
coefficients are the real fold-3 values of our plain logit **SC-02**
(`experiments/scorecard/SC-02` / `SC-03/explain.md`). This is for learning, not the final model:
it skips the winsorisation step.

```python
# Rossi Srl: from raw accounts to a PD with the SC-02 logit (fold 3, trained on 2007-2009)
import numpy as np

acct = dict(asst_tot=4_000_000, eqty_tot=300_000, debt_st=2_400_000, cash=40_000,
            profit=-120_000, ebitda=150_000, exp_financing=100_000, AR=900_000,
            revenue=3_000_000, tang_fixed=600_000)

slog = lambda v: np.sign(v) * np.log1p(abs(v))          # "signed log": tames huge ratios
x = {
    "log_assets":         np.log(acct["asst_tot"]),
    "equity_ratio":       acct["eqty_tot"] / acct["asst_tot"],
    "st_debt_ratio":      acct["debt_st"] / acct["asst_tot"],
    "cash_ratio":         acct["cash"] / acct["asst_tot"],
    "roa_net":            acct["profit"] / acct["asst_tot"],
    "interest_coverage":  slog(acct["ebitda"] / acct["exp_financing"]),
    "interest_to_assets": acct["exp_financing"] / acct["asst_tot"],
    "ar_days":            slog(365 * acct["AR"] / acct["revenue"]),
    "tangible_ratio":     acct["tang_fixed"] / acct["asst_tot"],
    "neg_equity":         float(acct["eqty_tot"] < 0),
}
beta = {"const": -5.5957, "log_assets": 0.0124, "equity_ratio": -0.352, "st_debt_ratio": 1.1799,
        "cash_ratio": -0.3173, "roa_net": -9.2162, "interest_coverage": -0.065,
        "interest_to_assets": 15.078, "ar_days": 0.0818, "tangible_ratio": -1.0059,
        "neg_equity": 0.8349}            # manufacturing / SRL / North-West = reference levels (0)

def pd_of(x):
    z = beta["const"] + sum(beta[k] * v for k, v in x.items())
    return z, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

z, p = pd_of(x)
print(f"score (log-odds) = {z:.3f}  ->  PD = {p:.2%}")
for k, v in x.items():
    print(f"  {k:18s} value {v:9.3f}   contribution {beta[k]*v:+.3f}")

# what-if: the same firm makes a +3% profit instead of a -3% loss
x2 = dict(x, roa_net=+0.03)
print(f"if profitable: PD = {pd_of(x2)[1]:.2%}")
```

What to notice:
- Each line is "coefficient × value". The biggest pushes come from short-term debt (+0.71),
  receivable days (+0.38), interest burden (+0.38) and the loss (+0.28).
- Turning the loss into a profit drops the PD from 1.98% to 1.15%.
- `log_assets` has a tiny **positive** coefficient. That's the "wrong" sign: bigger should be
  safer. It's not significant and it flips between folds (D-022), which is a known weakness of
  the plain logit.

## 4. The features

### 4.1 Why ratios, not raw euros

A €1M loss is a disaster for a €2M firm and noise for a €500M firm. Dividing by total assets puts
every firm on the same scale. Size enters exactly once, as `log_assets` (Roger M9: raw amounts
are never used directly).

### 4.2 Families (borrowed from RiskCalc)

RiskCalc v3.1 (R-01 Sec. 3.1) picks ratios from families: **profitability, leverage, debt
coverage, liquidity, activity, size, growth**. One or two per family; too many ratios overfit. We
can't do growth (that needs last year's statement, D-020), so we added **asset structure**.

### 4.3 Every feature in plain words

**How to read "AUC alone":** how well that one ratio ranks firms by itself, on 2007–09 training
rows. Above 0.5 means a higher value is riskier; below 0.5 means a higher value is safer (0.26 is
as strong as 0.74, just pointing the other way).

**"PD at low / typical / high value":** from the RiskCalc model's curves (fold 3,
`experiments/riskcalc/RC-03/explain.md`). Bad firm (5th percentile), typical firm (median), good
firm (95th percentile).

| Feature | Formula | Plain meaning | Expected | AUC alone | PD at low / typical / high value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **roa_net** ⭐ | net profit / assets | Is the firm making or losing money? | higher → safer | 0.256 | ROA −6.7% → **3.6%**; 0.3% → 0.9%; 10.7% → 0.45% |
| **equity_ratio** ⭐ | equity / assets | How much cushion before lenders lose money | higher → safer | 0.267 | 0.3% → **2.1%**; 18% → 0.9%; 83% → 0.45% (negative equity: up to 16%) |
| **st_debt_ratio** ⭐ | short-term liabilities / assets | Bills due within a year vs what you own | higher → riskier | 0.706 | 2% → 0.45%; 48% → 1.0%; 95% → **2.7%** |

| **interest_coverage** ⭐ | slog(EBITDA / interest) | How many times earnings cover interest | higher → safer | 0.276 | −7.6× → **5.0%**; 2.9× → 0.8%; 560× → 0.5% |
| **interest_to_assets** ⭐ | interest paid / assets | Interest burden (cost of debt × leverage) | higher → riskier | 0.634 | 0% → 0.8%; 1.3% → 1.0%; 4.6% → **2.6%** |

### 4.4 Features we rejected, and why (be ready to say this)

| Rejected | Why |
|---|---|
| `roe` (file and recomputed) | Blank or explodes when equity ≤ 0, exactly the riskiest firms; covered by roa_net + equity_ratio + neg_equity |
| `days_rec` (file) | 72% missing, inconsistent → replaced by our own ar_days |
| tax / assets | `taxes` is really pre-tax profit → duplicate of ROA (correlation 0.96) |
| inc_financing / assets | Field meaning unclear; exp_financing is unambiguous |
| goodwill / assets | It's really value added; can't explain it to a credit officer under that name |
| log revenue | A second size measure; size enters once |
| long-term debt / assets | Ambiguous direction, weak |
| Growth / last-year features | Need another row; gained only +0.002 AUC, all on firms already seen (D-020) |
| Raw euro amounts, raw province / division codes | Not comparable; too few defaults per code |
| NLP, macro data | No text in the data; external data not cleared (D-003, D-004) |

### 4.5 Cleaning rules (what happens to every ratio)

1. **Rebuild missing inputs from accounting identities first.** Example: if `debt_st` is
   missing, it equals current assets − net working capital.
2. **Safe division:** total assets is always ≥ €1.5M, so most ratios divide by it. Fragile
   denominators get bounded alternatives (equity/assets instead of debt/equity) or a documented
   rule (EBITDA / zero interest → "very high coverage" → capped).
3. **Signed log** for heavy-tailed ratios (`ar_days`, `ap_days`, `interest_coverage`,
   `current_ratio`, `ebitda_margin`): sign(x)·ln(1+|x|). It keeps the order but stops one crazy
   value from dominating a linear model.
4. **Winsorise:** cap at the 1st/99th percentile **of the training years**. Moody's capped at
   2nd/98th in RiskCalc v1 (R-05 p. 55).
5. **Debt breakdown missing:** fill each piece as (training sector median share) × (the firm's
   own debt total), plus the flag `debt_split_missing`. This beat a plain median: the bank-debt
   ratio's AUC on those rows was 0.553 vs 0.462.
6. **Categories:** pool any level with < 30 training defaults into OTHER; unseen levels → OTHER.
   Never treat a code as a number.

**A known weakness (Moody's, R-34):** filling missing values with an average tends to make PDs
**too low**. The GLMs fill remaining gaps with sector medians; LightGBM leaves them as NaN and
learns what "missing" means. Checking this is on the to-do list.

## 5. The models

All four families use the same features and folds and are scored the same way. Results are
pooled over 2008–2010 (`reports/tournament/scorecard_r1.md`).

### 5.1 Scorecard family (the benchmarks): "simple and transparent"

**SC-01 one-ratio logit.** Use only the single best ratio (ROA was chosen in every fold).
AUC **0.746**. That's the floor: "how much does everything else add?"

**SC-02 plain logit.** The worked example in Section 3: one coefficient per ratio, add up,
convert. AUC **0.803**.
- **Weakness:** it assumes every ratio acts in a straight line on the log-odds. Real curves bend:
  equity at 0% vs 5% matters hugely, 60% vs 65% hardly at all.
- `log_assets` gets the wrong sign.

**SC-03 WoE scorecard (the benchmark, AUC 0.817).**
1. Cut each ratio into bins learned on the training years (about 20 bins, merged so risk only
   moves one way).
2. Give each bin its **Weight of Evidence**: WoE = ln(share of defaulters in the bin / share of
   survivors in the bin). Positive = riskier than average.
   Real example (`SC-03/explain.md`):
   - Equity ratio ≤ 0.28%: 23,793 firms, 1,860 defaults (7.8% vs 1.3% overall) → WoE +1.86.
   - Equity ratio around 35–41%: default rate 0.47% → WoE −1.01.
3. Run a logit on the WoE values. Convert to **points** (like a credit card score): each bin is
   worth a number of points, and you add them up.
- **Why it beats the plain logit:** the bins capture bends in the curves.
- **Strengths:** a loan officer can do it on paper; no wrong signs.
- **Weakness:** steps instead of smooth curves; no interactions.

**SC-04 Altman-style.** Altman's Z-score ratios. AUC **0.770**. The original Z needs the stock
market value of equity (private firms don't have one) and retained earnings (not in our data), so
it's a lineage benchmark only.

### 5.2 RiskCalc-style (RC-01..03): "Roger's own design"

PD = F( Φ( Σ βᵢ·Tᵢ(ratioᵢ) + industry ) ) (R-01 Sec. 3.1 p. 14). In words:

1. **Tᵢ, a curve per ratio:** for each ratio, learn "ratio value → default rate" from the
   training data (bins, smoothed, forced to move one way). Example: ROA −6.7% → 3.6%,
   +10.7% → 0.45%. **Missing values get their own default rate** (missing ar_days → 5.9%).
2. **Probit:** add the curves (on a probit scale) with weights β, plus industry dummies. Probit is
   logit's twin with a slightly different S-curve; tested, near-identical (D-043).
3. **F, a final map:** turn the score into a PD, fitted on the training years.

Results:
- **RC-01** (10 core ratios): 0.818.
- **RC-02** (+ bank debt, payable days): **0.819**.
- **RC-03** (drop wrong-signed ratios): 0.819, but the rule was written after seeing test
  results → "CONCERN", so we report **RC-02**.

**Why it's good:**
- The **driver curves are the explanation**. "Firms with ROA of −7% default about 4× more often
  than typical firms" is exactly how a banker thinks.
- The same 10 ratios are used every year (Roger's M12 question).
- It's the method Roger built.

**Why it's not the winner:** it adds up separate curves, so it can't see combinations (e.g. a
loss is worse when there's also no equity cushion). It's only +0.002 AUC over the WoE scorecard.

**Reason code example** (`RC-03/explain.md`, 99th-percentile firm, PD 12.1%): "ROA of −15.4%
(typical 0.3%) raised this firm's PD from 3.5% to 12.1%."

### 5.3 LightGBM (GB-01..06): "the winner"

**What a boosted tree model is.** A tree is a chain of yes/no questions:

```
Is net profit / assets < -2%?
 ├─ yes: Is equity / assets < 5%?  ├─ yes: +0.9 to the score   └─ no: +0.3
 └─ no:  Is short-term debt / assets > 80%? ├─ yes: +0.2  └─ no: -0.2
```

Boosting builds **hundreds** of small trees. Each new tree fixes the mistakes of the ones before,
and the score is the sum of all tree outputs. Because one tree can ask about two ratios in a row,
the model **learns combinations** automatically. That's where the extra accuracy comes from.

**Monotone constraints** (D-051): we tell the model "for this ratio, more must never mean
safer" (or riskier), using the expected-sign table. Without them, trees can learn wiggles like
"more equity raises PD between 40% and 45%". That's noise, and a credit officer would reject it.

**Our settings:**
- All 26 features; missing values left as NaN (the model learns where "missing" belongs).
- Shallow trees and many firms per leaf (conservative, because defaults are rare).
- Tuning on the last training year only.
- No re-weighting of defaults.
- A Platt calibration step fitted inside the training years.

**Results:**

| Exp | What | AUC | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GB-01** | monotone, full features | **0.838** | Every ratio moves the expected way |
| GB-02 | no constraints | 0.844 | +0.0065, but working capital, interest coverage and bank debt move the **wrong** way → rejected |
| GB-03 | core features only | 0.830 | Extra features are worth +0.008 |
| GB-04 | median-fill instead of NaN | ≈ GB-01 | No difference |
| GB-05 | "advanced" monotone method | ≈ GB-01 | No gain |
| GB-06 | 3 constraints loosened | 0.838 | +0.0005 → constraints cost almost nothing where we doubted them |

**What drives it** (`GB-01/explain.md`, SHAP importance, by family): leverage 0.45, asset
structure 0.44, profitability 0.41, activity 0.40, coverage 0.28, industry 0.12, liquidity 0.09,
firm type 0.03, size 0.02, geography 0.01, missing flag 0.00.

**SHAP reason codes:** SHAP splits one firm's score into the push from each feature, and the
pushes add up exactly to the score. Example, the 99th-percentile firm (PD 13.9%):
- loss of −6.7% of assets: +0.93;
- equity −1% of assets: +0.50;
- short-term liabilities 89% of assets: +0.41.

**Caution when explaining:** correlated features share credit (ROA, cash flow, operating ROA,
EBITDA margin all measure profitability). Explain by **family**, not single ratio.

**Surprise to be ready for:** `tangible_ratio` (hard assets / total) is the #1 single driver. It
holds *within* every industry (within-group AUC 0.56–0.73), so it isn't just "real estate is
safe". Story: tangible assets are collateral and hold value in distress.

**Known weaknesses:**
- **New firms:** AUC 0.787 vs 0.844 for firms seen in training.
- **A capped reason code:** for firms with almost no sales, receivable days hits its cap (about
  60,000 days). The median example firm is one of these, so its reason code really means
  "almost no revenue".
- **Less transparent globally** than RiskCalc's curves, though SHAP dependence plots exist
  (`GB-01/dep_*.png`).

### 5.4 EBM / GAM (GAM-01, GAM-02): "the best fully explainable model"

An **Explainable Boosting Machine** sits between RiskCalc and LightGBM.
- **One curve per feature**, like RiskCalc, so you can plot exactly how each ratio moves the
  PD, and the curves add up to the score.
- The curves are learned by **boosting, one feature at a time** (round-robin), on all 26
  features, with the same direction rules as LightGBM.
- **GAM-02** also adds 10 two-feature "interaction" curves.

**Results** (`experiments/gam/`, bootstrap in `paired_bootstrap.csv`):

| | AUC pooled | AUC 2010 | Log loss after calibration | vs LightGBM |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GAM-01 (curves only) | 0.825 | 0.818 | 0.0645 | **−0.012 AUC [−0.014, −0.010]** |
| GAM-02 (+10 interactions) | 0.826 | 0.819 | 0.0644 | interactions add only +0.0008 [+0.0004, +0.0012] |

**What it teaches us:**
- The EBM beats RiskCalc-style (0.819) and the scorecard (0.817), so it's the **most accurate
  model where every feature's effect is one readable curve**.
- It still trails LightGBM by 0.012. So **LightGBM's edge comes from combinations of ratios**
  that a curve-per-feature model can't represent. Even 10 interaction pairs recover only about
  7% of the gap.

**Shapes** (all 20 features with an expected direction obey it):
- **Tangible ratio:** PD falls almost in a straight line as hard assets rise.
- **Short-term debt:** PD rises smoothly; negative equity adds a further jump.
- **Profitability (ROA, cash flow, EBITDA margin):** PD falls steeply **until zero**, then flat.
  Losses are the signal; extra profit adds nothing.

**Quirks to know:**
- `rev_nonpositive = 1` (no sales) *lowers* risk, which an officer won't believe alone. These
  are probably holding / real-estate companies.
- Pooled "OTHER" category levels get large effects.
- One fix was needed: a "missing" bin learned from 19 rows with 0 defaults scored −5.1 log-odds,
  which would price a messy row at about 1/150 of the average PD. Missing bins with < 30 training
  defaults are now reset to neutral (D-082).

**Practical:** prediction is fast (about 0.9 s per 100k rows). The saved models depend on
the `interpret` library unless exported to tables.

## 6. Calibration: getting the level right

### 6.1 The problem

Every model's average PD was too low:

| Test year | Model's average PD vs actual (GB-01) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 2008 | actual is 1.21× predicted | trained on 2007 (0.96%) |
| 2009 | 1.36× | trained on 2007–08 (about 1.1%) |
| 2010 | 1.43× | trained on 2007–09 (about 1.3%) |

The ranking was fine (calibration slope about 1). Only the **level** was off, because defaults
rose every year and a model learns the average of its past.

### 6.2 The fix: shift every firm's log-odds by the same amount

This is the **prior shift** (Saerens et al. 2002, eq. 4). Move all scores up by one number so the
average PD hits the target. Everyone moves up together, so the **ranking (AUC) is unchanged**
(verified).

```python
# prior shift: move the average PD from what training saw (r_w) to the target level (tau)
import numpy as np
logit = lambda p: np.log(p / (1 - p))
r_w, tau = 0.01409, 0.01493           # 2007-2010 fit rate -> recommended holdout level (D-073)
delta = logit(tau) - logit(r_w)       # one number, frozen in the harness
pd_raw = np.array([0.001, 0.005, 0.0198, 0.10, 0.40])
pd_new = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(logit(pd_raw) + delta)))
print(f"delta = {delta:+.3f}")
for a, b in zip(pd_raw, pd_new):
    print(f"  {a:.2%} -> {b:.2%}")
```

### 6.3 Which level, and why 1.49%

- In the backtest, the best rule was "default rate of the last 6 training months". It recovered
  88% of what a perfect, hindsight level would give (log loss 0.06415 → 0.06352). Caveat: we
  tried 7 rules, so this is a bit optimistic.
- For the holdout: the latest cohort (2011 statements, 11 of 12 months observed) runs at
  **1.43%**. We add a small hedge to **1.49%**, because **being too low is about 1.5× more costly
  than being too high** (30% too low costs 8–11e-4 log loss; 30% too high costs 6–7e-4).
- **Rejected:**
  - isotonic (slightly hurts ranking);
  - Platt as a level fix (it hides a trend guess);
  - EM estimation from the holdout batch (would use holdout data, and was wrong by up to 4×);
  - trend extrapolation (overshot 29% in 2011);
  - long-run average (worse);
  - a 50% cap (firms above 50% PD really default 65% of the time).
- **Floor 0.03%** (by analogy with the Basel corporate PD floor, R-15 para. 285). It affects 31
  firms, none of which defaulted. **Cap 99%.**

## 8. Questions you'll be asked, with answers

1. **"Why a 6-month gap before the default window?"** The bank can only lend on approved, filed
   accounts: up to 120/180 days for approval plus 30 for filing under Italian law. With 0 months
   the default rate is 1.07% instead of 1.41%, and we show the sensitivity.
2. **"Why not maximise AUC with the unconstrained model?"** It makes some ratios work backwards
   (more working capital → higher PD). That's overfitting noise, and a credit officer would
   reject the model. The constraints cost 0.0065 AUC and give zero violations.
3. **"Why is your #1 driver tangible assets?"** It's collateral that keeps value in distress. It
   holds within every industry, so it isn't an industry proxy.
4. **"How do AUC and AR differ?"** They don't: AR = 2·AUC − 1. We report AUC.
5. **"Is your improvement real?"** +0.021 AUC with a 95% CI of [+0.019, +0.023], from a bootstrap
   that resamples firms. On the clean 2010 year: +0.019 [+0.016, +0.023].
6. **"Your PDs were too low. Why should we trust the level now?"** Default rates rose every year,
   so any model trained on the past is low. We shift the level using only information available at
   decision time; it fixed 2008–09 almost perfectly (1.02, 1.05) and most of 2010 (1.18). Being
   too low costs more, so we hedge up slightly.
7. **"How do you handle missing data?"** First rebuild from accounting identities. Then the debt
   breakdown (missing as a block, likely abbreviated accounts) is filled from sector shares of the
   firm's own debt, with a flag. Blanks that mean "negative equity" are never averaged away.
8. **"Does each year's model use different variables?"** No. One fixed feature set; only the
   parameters are re-estimated each year.
9. **"What about new firms?"** They're ranked less well (0.787 vs 0.844). It's on the limitations
   slide; the model never uses firm identity.
10. **"How would a loan officer use this?"** PD → grade (table above) → price band. Each PD comes
    with its top drivers in words.
11. **"Why not RiskCalc itself?"** We built a RiskCalc-style model and it's our explainable
    benchmark (0.819). Trees beat it by 0.018 because they see combinations of ratios.
12. **"Is this data only booked loans?"** We can't verify it. The coverage looks like a broad
    sample of Italian firms, so we state it as a limitation and an assumption.
