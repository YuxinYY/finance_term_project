## Literature Review

Our design follows the practitioner approach to private-firm default prediction, best documented in Moody's RiskCalc (Dwyer, Kocagil & Stein, 2004; Falkenstein, Boral & Carty, 2000). RiskCalc transforms each financial ratio, combines the results in a probit model with industry effects, and then maps the score to a calibrated PD. Its ratio families (leverage, profitability, coverage, growth, liquidity, activity and size) guide our feature set. We rely on the French edition of RiskCalc 4.0 (Buitrago et al., 2015), because no Italian edition was available to us. Evaluation follows Stein (2007), who prefers out-of-time testing and warns that reusing test years during development invalidates the result. That is why every parameter, including imputation values and scalers, is fitted inside the training window of each walk-forward round. Statements also reach lenders late, so we assume a five-month lag: Italian law allows 120 days for approval and 30 more for filing (Codice Civile, arts. 2364 and 2435), and Moody's reports three to four months in US data. Two choices rest on weaker evidence. Bryzgalova et al. (2025)† show that missing financial data follow systematic patterns, which supports our missing-value flags, though their setting is stock-market firms. Kim, Cho and Ryu (2021)† report that geometrically decaying lags help machine-learning default models, yet Falkenstein et al. (2000) find the latest year beats a three-year average. We therefore treat the decay-weighted aggregation as an empirical choice to be tested, not as established practice.

## Feature Importance

We measure feature importance with SHAP values (Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Lundberg et al., 2020). Each firm's predicted log-odds is split into one contribution per feature, and the contributions sum exactly to the model output. Averaging their absolute values gives a global ranking. We prefer this to built-in split-count or gain importance, which Lundberg et al. show can be inconsistent. In our reference gradient-boosting model, the largest drivers in 2010 were the tangible-asset ratio, asset turnover, short-term debt share and net ROA. Three cautions apply. Importance is not causation, and correlated features share credit. The tangible-asset effect appears in our data, but we found no study showing that tangibility lowers PD. Finally, forcing monotone signs aids explanation without improving accuracy. In our tests the unconstrained twin ranked firms slightly better out of time. For the final model, we will report SHAP rankings by walk-forward round and check that the top features stay stable.

## PR-AUC versus ROC-AUC

PR-AUC is permitted, because the assignment judges probability accuracy and discriminatory power without naming metrics. It averages precision over all recall levels, so it concentrates on the rare defaulters (Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015). That is its benefit. Its cost is that the score depends on the default rate: the baseline of a precision-recall curve is the share of positives. Our annual default rate moved from 1.28% to 1.70% over 2008–2010, so PR-AUC values from different years are not comparable. It also ignores the PD level, which matters for pricing. ROC-AUC does not change with the default rate, and it is the measure used in the validation literature we cite (Stein, 2007). The two curves agree on which model dominates (Davis & Goadrich, 2006)†, but their areas can rank close models differently. We therefore keep AUC and log loss as headline metrics and report PR-AUC as a within-year supplement, with the default rate shown beside it.

## Calibration

The team's calibration has two steps. First, we rank firms by score, group them into buckets, and fit a curve from score to observed default rate, as RiskCalc does in its final transformation. Second, we move the level from the training default rate to the target population rate using the prior-shift formula of Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker (2002). Two points need care. A boosted model scores its own training rows too confidently, so the curve should be fitted on a held-out slice, not the training rows (Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, 2005). The target rate must also be fixed in advance from training data, since the harness may not use the holdout. The formula assumes firm characteristics keep the same distribution when the default rate changes, which is doubtful across 2008–2012, so we use it only to set the level. In our reference run, observed defaults ran 21% to 43% above predictions in 2008–2010. One frozen level shift cut pooled log loss from 0.06415 to 0.06352 and left AUC unchanged. We will report calibration-in-the-large, slope, log loss and binned observed-versus-predicted rates for each walk-forward year.
